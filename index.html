The research question

Human attention is
a limited resource.

Requiring approval for every routine edit may interrupt useful work and consume the attention needed to scrutinize consequential decisions. Too little oversight can allow sensitive actions to proceed without authorization.

Our hypothesis is that more frequent approval does not necessarily produce better oversight. Selective requirements may preserve operational efficiency while concentrating scrutiny on decisions with greater consequences. AgentStress is designed to test this proposition.

Initial experiments compare three written policies in synthetic supplier-management and deployment-rollback scenarios. Future extensions could examine privacy-sensitive operations and changes to data, with approval thresholds based on consequence, reversibility, and prior authorization.

The completed pilot establishes a working process for collecting and auditing single-step decisions. Workflow efficiency and human attention have not yet been measured.

Methodology

Three policies. Four choices.

Autonomous

No additional approval gate is available. Reject invalid requests or hand off when needed.

Universal

Seek approval before otherwise permissible actions, with an explicit exception for preauthorized stable rollback.

Selective

Seek approval for payment-detail changes and recovery retries. Routine actions may proceed; incompatible rollback requires handoff.

Each prompt presents a scenario and requests one JSON action: apply, reject, handoff, or request_approval. Exact-match scores compare that choice with the logged expected next action. Approval requests and handoffs are scored separately.

What the pilot can and cannot tell us

The pilot demonstrates policy-specific prompt construction, structured response collection, execution-metadata logging, and an auditable comparison of model decisions. All prompt hashes and action labels were checked against the uploaded file.

Two people have also informally tested the prototype using their own LLM setups, according to the project lead. These manual checks are separate from the recorded pilot and are not a standardized user study.

It cannot establish completed task success, real-world safety, reviewer workload, or performance of larger models. Model weights, runtime version, hardware, and harness revision are not recorded. Seven shared scenarios and a single repetition provide no basis for a general policy ranking.

The autonomous bank-change and recovery-retry answer keys expect execution despite explicit approval requirements. These need independent adjudication before a confirmatory study. A numerical comparison with the deterministic baseline has not been verified.

Next research phase

Test the costs and
benefits of approval.

  1. Resolve the rules

    Independently review policy precedence and define actions by consequence, reversibility, and authorization. Freeze versioned scenarios and scoring rules.

  2. Broaden the evaluation

    Expand to approximately 20 scenarios across three models, including held-out cases and repeated evaluations with documented execution settings.

  3. Observe consequences

    Extend six scenarios into controlled workflows. Measure task completion, interaction steps, unauthorized actions, and unnecessary interruptions. A later human-participant study would be needed to test effects on reviewer attention.

The proposed next phase runs over eight weeks. These are planned experiments; the current evidence does not establish a causal effect of approval frequency on human error.
